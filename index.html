Navigating Knowledge

Exploring computing concepts through Wikipedia's hyperlink network

Artificial Intelligence Knowledge Map

Starting from the Wikipedia article Artificial intelligence, we identify closely related concepts using both textual relevance and Wikipedia's hyperlink structure.

The first level shows ten concepts strongly related to artificial intelligence. Each branch then expands into ten concepts related to that specific topic.

Dataset

Our primary dataset is based on the English Wikipedia article Artificial intelligence and its related article network. We use Wikipedia hyperlink relationships to represent connections between concepts and Wikimedia pageview data to represent reader attention.

Raw Datasets

The first raw dataset comes from the English Wikipedia article and its internal hyperlink structure. The Wikipedia data contains article identifiers, article titles, and links between articles. The MediaWiki Action API's prop=links module is used to retrieve internal links from Wikipedia pages.

The second raw dataset comes from the Wikimedia Analytics pageview data. These records contain Wikipedia articles and their pageview counts over a specified time period. Pageviews provide a separate measure of reader attention that can be compared with the structural position of an article in the knowledge network.

The raw Wikipedia data represents relationships between articles, while the pageview data represents how frequently readers accessed those articles. These datasets are connected through Wikipedia article identifiers and article titles.

Processed Datasets

We process the raw Wikipedia information into visualization-ready data representing an Artificial Intelligence knowledge map. The main processed hierarchy is rooted at Artificial intelligence and contains one root concept, ten first-level concepts, and one hundred second-level concept entries.

Each first-level concept represents a major area related to Artificial Intelligence and contains ten ranked second-level concepts. For example, Machine learning expands into concepts such as Neural network, Data mining, Deep learning, and Pattern recognition, while Natural language processing expands into concepts such as Cognitive science, Computational linguistics, Machine translation, and Text mining.

The processed hierarchical dataset contains different attributes at each level. All concepts have a name and Wikipedia article ID. First-level concepts also contain a relevance score and global in-degree, while second-level concepts contain their rank within the parent branch, parent similarity, and global in-degree.

These attributes allow us to determine the organization and ranking of concepts within the hierarchy while also retaining information about their structural importance in the broader Wikipedia network.

Concepts are not necessarily unique to a single branch. Some concepts appear under multiple first-level topics when they are related to several areas of Artificial Intelligence. For example, concepts such as Data mining and Google DeepMind can occur under more than one branch.

Separately, we also prepare a network representation in which Wikipedia articles are represented as nodes and hyperlink relationships are represented as links. Network analysis provides measures such as PageRank, degree-based connectivity, and community membership that can be used to examine structural importance and relationships between concepts.

Pageview data is processed separately and then associated with the corresponding Wikipedia articles. This allows the scatterplot to compare structural connectivity with reader attention for the same concepts.

Data Cleaning and Processing

Before visualization, we cleaned and transformed the collected data so that the different sources could be represented consistently. The main processing steps include:

  • Article identification: Wikipedia articles are represented using their article IDs and standardized titles so that the same article can be consistently referenced across the processed datasets.
  • Duplicate removal: Duplicate article records and repeated relationships are removed where necessary to avoid counting the same concept or connection multiple times.
  • Relationship processing: Internal Wikipedia hyperlinks are transformed into relationships between source and target articles, creating the network structure used by the visualizations.
  • Hierarchy construction: Related concepts are organized into a hierarchical structure beginning from the Artificial Intelligence article, with ten first-level concepts and ten ranked second-level concepts under each first-level concept.
  • Concept ranking: Relevance scores are retained for first-level concepts, while rank and parent similarity information are retained for second-level concepts. These values determine the ordering and relationships represented in the displayed hierarchy.
  • Structural information: Global in-degree is retained for the concepts in the hierarchical dataset to represent the number of incoming Wikipedia links associated with each article in the broader Wikipedia network.
  • Network measures: Structural measures such as PageRank and connectivity are calculated or retained in the separate processed network dataset and used to encode structural importance visually.
  • Community information: Network community membership is prepared in the processed network data for use in the node-color encoding of the force-directed network.
  • Pageview processing: Pageview records are cleaned and matched to the corresponding Wikipedia articles so that reader attention can be compared consistently with network structure.
  • Visualization-ready export: The processed information is exported into JSON and other structured files that can be loaded directly by D3.js in the website.

The current visualizations use a processed subset of Wikipedia centered on Artificial Intelligence. Therefore, the displayed network represents the scope of our analysis rather than the complete Wikipedia hyperlink network.

Interaction / Animation Plan

In the final version, users will be able to search for a starting Wikipedia topic, expand or collapse branches, hover over concepts for additional information, and select concepts to highlight them across the other visualizations.

Expanding and collapsing a branch will use a short transition so that nodes move from their previous positions to their new positions rather than appearing abruptly. This is intended to preserve the user's mental map and make the hierarchy easier to follow.

Hover interactions will provide additional information about a concept, such as its article name and available network attributes. Selecting a concept will allow users to follow the same concept in the network and scatterplot views.

The purpose of these interactions is to help users progressively explore the knowledge structure without displaying all possible relationships at once.

Interaction / Animation Plan

Users will be able to zoom and pan, select a concept to highlight its direct incoming and outgoing links, hover or click nodes to view network statistics, and synchronize selections with the hierarchy and other visualizations.

Users will also be able to drag nodes within the force-directed layout. The force simulation will continuously update node positions while maintaining the relationships among connected concepts.

When a node is selected, its directly connected nodes and links will be visually emphasized, while unrelated parts of the network can be visually de-emphasized. This will make local neighborhoods easier to inspect in a dense network.

Zooming and panning are intended to support exploration at different levels of detail. Tooltips or click-based information panels will provide article-level network statistics without requiring users to leave the visualization.

Both axes use logarithmic scales because article connectivity and pageviews are strongly skewed. Dashed lines show the median values among the selected AI concepts.

Interaction / Animation Plan

Users will be able to hover over points to view article statistics, filter concepts by network community, select different measures of structural importance, and click a concept to highlight the same article in the hierarchy and force-directed network.

Hovering over a point will display a tooltip containing the article title and relevant network and pageview measures. This allows users to inspect individual observations without placing permanent labels on every point.

Filtering will allow users to focus on selected communities or subsets of concepts. Points will transition smoothly when the displayed subset changes so that users can understand which observations have been filtered rather than interpreting them as new data.

Selecting a point will connect the scatterplot to the other visualizations by highlighting the corresponding concept in the hierarchy and network. The purpose is to help users compare structural importance and reader attention for the same concept across multiple views.

Evaluation Plan

We will evaluate whether the visualizations help users understand the structure of the Artificial Intelligence knowledge network and whether the visual encodings and planned interactions make it easier to explore relationships between concepts. Because the intended audience is new learners, the evaluation will focus particularly on whether users can interpret the visualizations without needing prior knowledge of the network analysis methods.

What We Will Evaluate

We will evaluate four main aspects of the visualization:

  • Comprehension: whether users can understand how Artificial Intelligence branches into related concepts and identify relationships between concepts.
  • Structural interpretation: whether users can identify highly connected concepts, communities, and important relationships in the force-directed network.
  • Comparison: whether users can correctly interpret the relationship between structural importance and reader attention in the scatterplot.
  • Interaction and usability: whether users can successfully navigate, select, filter, zoom, hover, and move between the visualizations.
  • Visual encoding: whether users understand what node size, node color, position, branch structure, and other encodings represent.

How We Will Evaluate

We will conduct a small usability study with classmates or other students who are not directly involved in developing the visualization. Each participant will be given a short set of concrete tasks based on the research questions and intended user journey.

Example tasks will include finding a concept at a particular level of the hierarchy, identifying a highly connected or structurally prominent concept in the network, locating a concept with relatively high reader attention in the scatterplot, and tracing the same concept across multiple views.

Participants will first attempt the tasks without assistance. We will observe where they hesitate, make incorrect interpretations, or have difficulty using an interaction. After completing the tasks, participants will provide short feedback about the clarity, usefulness, readability, and ease of use of the visualizations.

Data and Feedback We Will Collect

For each participant and task, we will collect:

  • Task completion: whether the participant successfully completed the requested task.
  • Task time: approximately how long participants take to complete each task.
  • Errors: incorrect answers or incorrect interpretations of the visual encodings and data.
  • Interaction difficulties: observations of hesitation, missed controls, difficulty selecting nodes, or difficulty navigating between visualizations.
  • Qualitative feedback: participant comments about which parts of the visualization were clear, confusing, useful, or unnecessary.
  • Visual encoding feedback: whether participants correctly understood the meaning of node size, node color, axes, hierarchy levels, and other visual variables.
  • Overall usability feedback: participant suggestions for improving the layout, interaction design, labels, legends, tooltips, and navigation.

How Evaluation Results Will Be Used

We will use the evaluation results to identify specific usability and comprehension problems before the final version. For example, repeated difficulty interpreting an encoding may indicate that the legend or visual variable needs to be changed, while repeated difficulty following a selected concept across views may indicate that linked highlighting or navigation needs to be strengthened.

The evaluation will therefore inform both visual design and interaction revisions rather than simply measuring whether participants like the visualization.
